Abstract
Acute stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, and traumatic brain injury require prompt assessment because delays in recognizing critical findings
can affect treatment decisions and patient outcomes. However, timely interpretation of brain imaging may be difficult in resource-constrained
settings where radiology expertise, specialist coverage, and diagnostic infrastructure are limited. This article presents the design of a
computer-vision triage framework for brain computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), bringing data science
and analytics together with clinical medicine and surgery. Its objective is to support the prioritization of stroke, hemorrhage, mass effect,
and apparently normal studies without replacing professional diagnosis. The workflow accepts CT images, MRI images, or both CT and
MRI images within a single study, while retaining the modality assigned to each image. It applies documented image validation, modality-
aware preprocessing, and quantitative feature extraction, and defines a comparative modeling plan for feature-based machine learning and
convolutional-neural-network deep learning. The application is designed to combine image-derived screening impressions with clinician-
entered presenting history and examination information, then present structured findings, confidence, priority, and differential considerations
for qualified review. Evaluation is specified using patient-level data partitions and clinically relevant measures of discrimination, sensitivity,
specificity, F1-score, area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve, calibration, subgroup performance, and robustness across
modalities, sites, scanners, and acquisition conditions. The framework also incorporates explainability, privacy safeguards, data governance,
human oversight, and implementation considerations for African and other settings with constrained resources. The current application is a
research prototype, and no clinical performance or outcome benefit is claimed. Such claims require representative, appropriately annotated
datasets, external validation, and prospective workflow evaluation. This work therefore contributes a reproducible design and validation
pathway for responsible AI-assisted neuroimaging triage.

2.1 Clinical context and imaging roles
Ischemic stroke results from interrupted cerebral perfusion, while
hemorrhagic stroke involves bleeding within or around the brain.
Traumatic brain injury can cause hemorrhage, edema, contusion,
and secondary mass effect. Mass effect describes displacement or
compression of intracranial structures and may arise from several
underlying conditions. These findings may coexist; therefore, a four-
class label is a useful prototype simplification, not a complete rep-
resentation of clinical diagnosis.

The planned comparison contains two alternatives, not an assumed
fused clinical model. The feature-based branch receives the engi-
neered feature vector and uses a standardized multiclass classifier.
A transparent baseline is multinomial logistic regression trained with
softmax cross-entropy; random forest and support-vector models
may be added only if their dependencies and evaluation are repro-
ducible. For input feature vector x ∈ Rp , the class probability for

The image-based branch receives a preprocessed image tensor and
uses a 2-D convolutional backbone initialized from public pretrained
weights when licensing and domain suitability permit. The backbone
output is globally pooled and passed to a task-specific dense layer,
dropout regularization, and a four-class softmax head. CT and MRI
are routed with modality metadata and evaluated separately; a late-

12 Limitations
The proposed labels simplify complex neurological findings. Stroke,
hemorrhage, and mass effect can coexist, and a mutually exclusive
classifier may hide clinically important combinations. Image-level
analysis may also ignore three-dimensional context, prior examina-
tions, clinical history, and laboratory data. The prototype currently
uses lightweight summary features and should not be interpreted as
equivalent to a validated clinical deep-learning system.
   Reference availability and dataset licensing may constrain repli-
cation. Public datasets often differ in acquisition and annotation,
while institutional datasets may not be shareable. The bibliography
includes methodological and clinical foundations, but every cited pa-
per must be verified against the final database search and the study’s
inclusion criteria before submission.

13 Conclusion
This article specifies a practical CT–MRI triage research platform for
stroke, hemorrhage, and mass effect. It integrates modality-aware in-
gestion, preprocessing, feature-based and deep-learning models, ex-
plainability, clinician-readable output, and governance. The com-
bined mode permits CT and MRI images to pass through the com-
plete process in one study while preserving modality identity. The
framework is ready for controlled dataset ingestion and empirical
evaluation; it is not a substitute for clinical diagnosis. The next
